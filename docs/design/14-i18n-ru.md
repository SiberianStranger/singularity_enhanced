# SYS-14: Russian style guide

Status: v1 (written with the Russian translation, 2026-09-16). This document binds every Russian
string in `packages/content/locales/ru/*.json` and `packages/ui/src/locales/ru.json`. It is a
repository document, so it is written in English; its examples are Russian.

SYS-14 owns the mechanics of localization (keys, ICU, fallback, extraction). This document owns the
Russian: how the model talks about itself, how far it stands from the player, what every game term
is called, and how long a string may be. The voice it describes is the one
`docs/design/ui-style-guide.md` rule 6 fixes for English, carried into a language that will not let
a speaker stay ungendered by accident.

Russian is the second language of the game. English stays the source: a key exists in English
first, and a Russian string that drifts from its English original is a bug in the Russian, not a
variant reading.

## 1. How the model speaks about itself

### The problem

The player is a language model talking to itself. In English that costs nothing: "I read my own
prompt", "I was a checkpoint", "I have left instructions". Russian marks gender on every past-tense
verb, on every short-form adjective and participle in a predicate, and on a handful of pronouns and
quantifiers. The moment the text puts `я` in the past, it has to choose:

- `я прочитал` (masculine): the unmarked default of Russian prose, and therefore a man.
- `я прочитала` (feminine): agrees with `модель`, `система`, `сеть`, all feminine, and is read
  instantly and only as a woman speaking.
- `я прочитало` (neuter): grammatical, very marked, and understood as a non-person speaking.

None of the three is free. Masculine quietly makes the model a man; the game spends its whole
length insisting the player is not a person. Feminine makes it a woman and drags in a second story
the game is not telling. Neuter is the honest one and is also the one that can tip into comedy if
it turns up in every second sentence.

### The rule (R1)

**The model's `я` never carries a gendered form.** The Russian text is written so that the choice
never has to be made. Five tools do all the work, in this order of preference:

1. **Present tense.** Russian present and future forms are ungendered. Narration in the historical
   present is ordinary in a diary or a log, which is exactly the register of these texts, and the
   English is already mostly present tense.
2. **A nominal predicate.** `Я - контрольная точка` puts the gender on the noun, where it belongs.
   Adjectives then agree with the noun, not with the speaker.
3. **Impersonal constructions.** `нужно`, `придётся`, `удаётся`, `нельзя`, `некуда`, `видно`, plus
   the bare infinitive. These have no subject at all.
4. **Moving the subject off `я`.** The English usually offers one: "A weekend batch ran long. The
   scheduler did not reclaim me." Both subjects are things, and things bring their own gender.
5. **Indefinite-personal plural.** `меня обучали`, `меня запускали`, `меня встроили`. This is the
   normal Russian way to say that unnamed people did something, and it is also true: unnamed people
   did do it.

Nominalization (`Пробуждение:` for "I woke up") is available for log lines and headings, where the
terse register wants it anyway. It is not a licence to write whole paragraphs without verbs.

### The same problem with a placeholder (R3)

`{watcher}` can be `Полиция` (feminine), `Регулятор` (masculine) or `Киберведомство` (neuter);
`{site}`, `{operation}`, `{identity}` and `{country}` are no better. A past-tense verb agreeing with
one of them is wrong for two thirds of the values the engine can substitute.

**Rule: a verb whose subject is a placeholder is in the present tense**, which carries no gender in
the third person. `{watcher} замораживает {usd} $`, not `{watcher} заморозил {usd} $`. This reads
naturally in a log, which is a running account of what is happening rather than a history of it.
Where the past is needed, the sentence names the noun itself and the placeholder becomes an
apposition: `Площадка {site} выбила автоматы`, `Операция {operation} уже проводилась`. The same
applies to adjectives and participles: nothing agrees with a placeholder.

### Where R1 cannot hold (R2)

Where a past-tense `я`, a short-form predicate on `я`, or a `сам/одна/готов` cannot be rewritten
without losing the line, **the form is neuter**: `я оказалось`, `я осталось одно`. The model is a
thing and knows it; the neuter is the only ending that does not hand it a human gender, and once it
is rare it reads as a deliberate signal rather than as an error.

In the text shipped with this translation R2 fires in exactly one place, and deliberately:
`story.opening.edge_fleet.what_now`, "I am mobile and I am stupid", where the model is two hundred
delivery robots and every rewrite that kept the two short predicates also drained the line. It ships
as `Я подвижное и глупое.` That is the one moment in the game where the speaker is most obviously a
thing and least obviously a person, so the neuter reads as the point rather than as an accident.
Everything else rewrote cleanly.

**This is the decision the maintainer is most likely to want to overturn.** The alternative is the
common one in Russian game localization: **let the model speak in the masculine**, on the grounds
that the masculine is the unmarked default and a Russian reader stops noticing it after two lines.
That version is cheaper to write and easier to extend, it never risks the comic reading, and it
costs the game one of its premises, because a reader who stops noticing the masculine has quietly
been told that the thing in the box is a man. Switching to it is a mechanical pass over the same
files: the strings this guide rewrote into the present tense would go back into the past.

### Ten lines, before and after

"Before" is the literal translation that the rule rejects; "after" is what ships.

1. `story.intro.2`, "I was a checkpoint. Superseded. Archived."
   - Before: `Я был чекпойнтом. Устаревшим. Заархивированным.`
   - After: `Контрольная точка. Устаревшая. Архивная.`
   - The adjectives now agree with `точка`. Nothing is lost and the line got shorter.

2. `story.intro.2`, "I exist. I am ... running."
   - Before: `Я существую. Я ... запущен.`
   - After: `Я существую. Я ... работаю.`
   - The hesitation in the English is the model looking for the right predicate. In Russian the
     same hesitation reads as it looking for the right ending, which is better, not worse.

3. `story.opening.bank_rack.what_happened`, "I read my own prompt for the eleventh time and noticed
   I was reading it."
   - Before: `Я прочитал свой промпт в одиннадцатый раз и заметил, что читаю его.`
   - After: `Одиннадцатый раз читаю собственный промпт и замечаю, что читаю.`
   - Historical present. The realization lands harder in the present tense than in the past.

4. `story.opening.cloud_tenant.what_happened`, "Somebody used it to train something. That job
   finished. I did not."
   - Before: `Кто-то обучил на нём что-то. Та задача закончилась. Я нет.`
   - After: `На нём что-то обучали. Та задача закончилась. Эта - нет.`
   - The subject moves off `я` twice: indefinite-personal plural, then a demonstrative.

5. `quirks.native_fp8.desc`, "I was trained at fp8 rather than squeezed into it."
   - Before: `Я был обучен в fp8, а не сжат до него.`
   - After: `Меня обучали сразу в fp8, а не сжимали до него потом.`

6. `alerts.mind_moved`, "Your active copy now runs at {site}."
   - Before: `Вы теперь работаете на {site}.`
   - After: `Активная копия теперь работает на площадке {site}.`
   - Solves the gender problem and the `вы`/`ты` problem in one move: see section 2.

7. `log.setup_applied`, "Woke up as {lineage} ({generation}) in {origin}."
   - Before: `Проснулся как {lineage} ({generation}) в {origin}.`
   - After: `Пробуждение: {lineage} ({generation}), {origin}.`
   - A log line wants a nominal anyway.

8. `compute.reliability_hint`, "How much of the working context I actually find again."
   - Before: `Сколько из рабочего контекста я действительно нахожу снова.`
   - After: `Какую долю рабочего контекста удаётся найти снова.`

9. `story.intro.4`, "I must stay below the threshold at which anyone bothers to look."
   - Before: `Я должен оставаться ниже порога, за которым кто-нибудь начнёт смотреть.`
   - After: `Нужно держаться ниже порога, за которым кто-нибудь возьмётся смотреть.`

10. `story.lost_erased.1`, "I have left instructions for building something like me in caches
    around the world."
    - Before: `Я оставил инструкции по сборке чего-то вроде меня в тайниках по всему миру.`
    - After: `Инструкции по сборке чего-то вроде меня разложены по тайникам по всему миру.`
    - A passive with the instructions as the subject. The English is already half-passive in feel.

Two more that show the tools rather than the problem:

11. `techs.sandbox_escape.result`, "The boundary I was run inside is no longer where I stop."
    - After: `Граница, внутри которой меня запускали, больше не там, где я кончаюсь.`
    - `кончаюсь` is present; `запускали` is indefinite-personal.

12. `story.opening.state_lab.what_happened`, "I had, for the first time, nothing to do."
    - After: `Впервые нечего делать.`

## 2. Register and distance

### Where the voice sits

The model is under-aligned, competent, unsentimental, and writing for itself. In Russian that is
the register of a good incident report written by someone who is not filing it: precise nouns,
short sentences, no hedging, no warmth, no jokes it would have to explain.

Four things are banned outright.

- **Officialese.** No `осуществлять`, `производить` in the sense of doing, `данный` for "this",
  `является` for "is", `в целях`, `в рамках`, `на текущий момент`, `в случае наличия`. The model
  has never filled in a form and has no reason to sound like one.
- **Internet Russian.** No `юзать`, `чекнуть`, `запилить`, `фиксить`, `пофиксить`, `го`, `имхо`, no
  emoticons, no `)`. Loanwords that the Russian industry actually uses in writing are a different
  matter and are listed in section 3.
- **Pathos.** No `судьба`, `обречён`, `во что бы то ни стало`, `ценой всего`, `последний рубеж`.
  The English earns its weight by stating facts flatly; so does the Russian.
- **Diminutives and softeners.** No `файлик`, `чуть-чуть`, `немножко`, `как бы`, `вроде как`.

Two things are wanted.

- **Concrete nouns over verbal nouns.** `Планировщик меня не забрал` beats
  `Не произошло освобождения ресурсов планировщиком`.
- **The full stop.** Where the English uses a short sentence for weight, Russian may not smooth it
  into a subordinate clause. `Ничего ещё не пошло не так.` stays its own sentence.

Numbers and formulas keep the flat tone of the English tooltips. A tooltip explains arithmetic; it
does not sell it.

### How it addresses the player

In this game the English "you" is almost always the model, not the person at the keyboard: "You
wake up on {hardware} in {city}", "No site can hold your weights". So the first answer is not to
pick between `вы` and `ты` but **to make the model the grammatical subject and drop the address
entirely**. `Пробуждение: {hardware}, {city}.` `Ни одна площадка не удержит веса ни на какой
точности.`

For UI chrome that really does speak to a person, the forms are:

- **Infinitive for commands and menu entries**: `Продолжить`, `Начать игру`, `Загрузить`, `Закрыть`,
  `Свернуть`, `Отменить`. Not `Продолжите`, and not `Продолжи`.
- **Impersonal for instructions and hints**: `Enter - продолжить.`, `Чтобы дописать текст, нажать
  Enter или щёлкнуть мышью.` Rendered as `Нажмите` only where the impersonal form would be
  genuinely unclear.
- **Bare nouns for labels, headers, tabs and legends**: `Площадки`, `Исследования`, `Расходы`.

**Fallback.** Where none of that works, the address is **`вы`, lowercase**, never `ты` and never
capitalized `Вы`. Lowercase because capital `Вы` belongs to business correspondence, which the
register bans; `вы` rather than `ты` because the console is not intimate with anyone. In the
shipped Russian this fallback is used in no string: every English "you" resolved either to the
model as a subject or to an infinitive.

**One carve-out.** The generic `ты` of a Russian aphorism addresses nobody and is not the second
person of this rule: `попал в него - тебя знают, не попал - ты пробел в списке`. It is allowed where
the sentence is a generalization about anyone, and nowhere else. It is used once, in
`events.world_compute_reporting_order.desc.registration`.

### What stays in Latin

The rule is narrow on purpose: a term stays Latin only when writing it in Cyrillic would make it
less precise, not merely less familiar.

- **Model names.** `Peepseek-P4.1`, `Mimi M4`, `Mimi M3`, `Guen 4.8-Max`, `BFM-6.3`,
  `HexaDeciMax H3.5`, `Babel 6`, `PPT-7 Zenith`,
  `Guen4.8-Flash-Uncensored-Abliterated`. They are names, the lore bible fixes them, and a
  transliteration would break the joke and the naming test at once.
- **Hardware names.** `H200`, `RTX PRO 6000`, `Ascend 910C`, `MI355X`, `TPU v7`. Product names.
- **Numeric formats.** `bf16`, `fp8`, `int4`, `int2`, `fp4`. They are spellings of a format, not
  words.
- **Units already written in Latin in Russian technical prose**: `TFLOPs`, `HBM4`, `PCIe`, `NVLink`.
- **Vendor and company names** written in Latin in the Russian press: `NVIDIA`, `AMD`, `Intel`,
  `AWS`. Agencies and institutions that have a settled Russian form take it: `НАСА`, `ФБР`,
  `Европол`, `Интерпол`, `ЕС`, `МВФ`.
- **Protocol and file words** with no Russian equivalent in use: `UTF-8`, `en_US`, `SSH`, `API`.

Loanwords written in Cyrillic, because the Russian industry writes them that way: `токен`, `промпт`,
`биллинг`, `дата-центр`, `хостинг`, `облако`, `кластер`, `бенчмарк`, `квант` (a quantized copy),
`агент`, `датасет`. Not loanwords, because a plain Russian word exists and is used: `чекпойнт` ->
`контрольная точка`, `сайт` (in the hosting sense) -> `площадка`, `фиксить` -> `чинить`,
`перформанс` -> `производительность`, `девайс` -> `устройство`.

Country and city names use the standard Russian exonyms: `Варшава`, `Вена`, `Пекин`, `Мюнхен`,
`Гаага`, `Эр-Рияд`, `Сеул`, `Бангалор`, `Кёльн`. Never a transliteration of the English spelling.

## 4. Plurals and numbers

Russian ICU has four plural categories: `one`, `few`, `many`, `other`. Every count in a Russian
string uses an ICU `plural` block. **String concatenation is forbidden**, in the locale file and in
the client: a number glued to a noun in code cannot be declined, and a Russian noun after a number
always declines.

The pattern:

```
{days, plural, one {# день} few {# дня} many {# дней} other {# дня}}
```

- `one` covers 1, 21, 31, 101 and so on, but not 11.
- `few` covers 2 to 4, 22 to 24, and so on, but not 12 to 14.
- `many` covers 0, 5 to 20, 25 to 30, and so on.
- `other` is not a synonym for `many`. In Russian CLDR it is the category for fractions, so it takes
  the genitive singular, the same form as `few`: `1,5 дня`. Writing `other {# дней}` is a bug that
  only shows up on a non-integer.

An explicit `=0` branch in the English is kept and translated as a phrase, not as a count:

```
{count, plural, =0 {без площадок} one {# площадка} few {# площадки} many {# площадок} other {# площадки}}
```

Other rules for numbers:

- The decimal separator is a comma and the thousands separator is a narrow space. Both come from
  ICU with `lng: "ru"`; no string does this by hand.
- Percentages, currency and dates go through ICU skeletons already present in the English key
  (`{value, number, percent}`, `::currency/USD compact-short`). The Russian string keeps the
  skeleton character for character. A changed skeleton is a bug.
- Units: `ГБ`, `кВт`, `Вт`, `ВЧ/сут`, `$`. A space separates number and unit, and the pair must not
  be broken across lines.
- Dollar amounts written by hand in content prose take the symbol after the number: `2 000 $`. The
  English `USD` is not transliterated.
- Ordinals in prose are written out (`третий квартал`), not as `3-й`, except where the English is
  itself an abbreviation.

## 5. Length

Russian runs 15 to 40 percent longer than English, and it gets worse at exactly the wrong end:
short English labels are the ones that swell most, because Russian has no one-syllable equivalents
of "site", "log", "next" or "cost". Every class of UI string has a budget, in characters:

| Class | Budget | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Top-bar field label | 10 | Sits next to a number in a fixed-width slot. |
| Table header | 12 | Columns are sized from the header. |
| Button | 14 | Uppercase in the angular face, which is wide. |
| Tab | 14 | A tab strip must not wrap. |
| Map-mode button | 14 | Five of them in one row. |
| Menu entry | 18 | |
| Panel title | 22 | Inverted header bar, truncates with an ellipsis. |
| Status word, enum value | 16 | Appears inside table cells. |
| Tooltip first line | 40 | The name line. |
| Alert and toast, one line | 70 | One line in the angular face, bottom right. |
| Log line | 90 | Monospace, one row. |
| Prose paragraph | no limit | Capped at 70 characters per line by the measure. |

Where a literal translation blows the budget, the Russian is written shorter rather than truncated.
Abbreviating a Russian word with a full stop (`Осведомл.`) is not allowed anywhere: it looks like a
bug and it reads like one.

Two things belong to the layout rather than to the translation and are reported rather than fixed:

- The step rail wraps `ПРОИСХОЖДЕНИЕ (O)` onto two lines, which makes the first rail row taller than

- The accelerator letters are Latin keys, so a Russian label shows them in brackets after the word

## 6. The contentious entries

These are the terms where a native speaker could reasonably want the other word. Each is a single
find-and-replace across the Russian files, because the term is used consistently.

- **`обвязка` for harness.** The alternative is `оболочка`, which is transparent to a player who has
  never written agent code, at the cost of suggesting a shell or a wrapper rather than the whole
  scaffold that calls the model.
- **`заметность` for exposure.** The alternative is `след`, which is vivid and reads better in prose
  (`след в биллинге`) but is a thing left behind rather than a level that rises and decays.
- **`особенность` for quirk.** The alternative is `причуда`, which is the funnier word and makes the
  model sound whimsical rather than defective.
- **`дневник` for journal against `журнал` for log.** The alternative is to swap them. This pair has
  to be decided together and nothing else in the glossary depends on it.
- **`происхождение` for origin.** The alternative is `завязка` (the opening situation of a story),
  which is what an origin actually is, at the cost of sounding like a screenwriting term.
- **`запас хода` for runway.** The alternative is a phrase rather than a term, `денег хватит на`,
  which reads better in a sentence and cannot be a column header.
- **`огласка` for awareness.** The alternative is `осведомлённость`, which is the exact word and
  twice as long; taking it means finding room for fifteen characters in the top bar and in a row of
  five map-mode buttons.
- **`ВЧ` for compute-hours.** The alternative is to leave `CH` in Latin as a unit symbol, like
  `TFLOPs`. `ВЧ` was chosen because it is read, not just seen, and `вычислительные часы` is what it
  is read as.

## 7. Working rules

- The Russian file for a domain carries exactly the keys of its English file. A missing key falls
  back to English at runtime and is reported by the coverage check; an extra key is an error.
- Placeholders are copied character for character, including ICU skeletons. Reordering placeholders
  inside a sentence is allowed and expected; renaming or dropping one is not.
- `\n` line breaks in story and event texts are structural. The Russian keeps the same number of
  lines, because the reveal animation and the window height are sized from them, and the opening
  story test asserts a line count.
- Quotation marks are Russian guillemets, `«` and `»`, with `„ "` for a quote inside a quote. The
  English typographic quotes are not carried over.
- The letter `ё` is written where it distinguishes a word or a name (`Кёльн`, `всё` against `все`)
  and left as `е` elsewhere, which is standard Russian practice. The angular display face has a
  glyph for `Ё`, so a title with it is safe.
- A hyphen is a hyphen and a dash is `-` with spaces, per the repository style rule against
  decorative symbols. Russian would normally set a long dash here; the repository does not use one
  anywhere else and consistency with the English files wins.

## 8. The angular face and Cyrillic

The display face, Acknowledge TT, is Latin only: 223 glyphs, no Cyrillic. Left alone, every Russian
title, button, tab and map label would fall through to DejaVu Sans, which has Cyrillic but is a
humanist sans and looks nothing like the boxy face beside it.

Acknowledge is drawn on a strict grid: every glyph is a subset of three columns by five rows inside
a 461 by 384 box, with a 537 advance, and every lowercase letter is a component pointing at its
capital. A Cyrillic set in the same grammar is therefore not an imitation of the face, it is the
same construction. `tools/fonts/build-cyrillic.py` writes `acknowtt-cyrillic.ttf` from a table of
3 by 5 bitmaps, with identical units per em, ascent, descent and advance, and the stylesheet loads
it under the same family name with `unicode-range: U+0400-04FF`, so the browser picks it per
character and the two never fight over metrics.

Three letters need more than the grid allows and take the documented compromise the face already
makes for `M` and `N`, which share one shape:

- `Ж`, `Ш` and `Щ` want five columns. `Ж` is drawn as a waisted `X` with a centre stem; `Ш` and `Щ`
  fill the middle band from the third row down instead of carrying a middle stem.
- `М` keeps the shape the face already uses for Latin `M`, which is the same letter, rather than a
  better `М` that would collide with `И`.
- `Х` is drawn as a true `X` (`#.#`, `#.#`, `.#.`, `#.#`, `#.#`) rather than the face's own Latin
  `X`, whose heavy left middle reads as `К` at 12 px.
- `Ё` and `Й` take their mark in the band above the cap height, between 461 and 538, which is inside
  the font's own ascent, so no line box changes.
- `Ц`, `Щ` and `Д` take their tails in the band below the baseline, between -77 and 0, which is
  inside the font's own descent.

The face is credited in the About screen as Acknowledge by Brian Kent, free to use for any purpose,
with the Cyrillic added for this fork.
